UNDERSTANDING THE ISSUE

WHAT IS MISINFORMATION?

Understanding false information, how it spreads, and its effects on society.

KEY DEFINITIONS

Misinformation

  • False or inaccurate information shared without the intention of causing harm.
  • People may share it because they believe it is true.
  • Example: Sharing an outdated weather warning because someone believes it is current.

Disinformation

  • False information deliberately created or shared to deceive people.
  • It can be used to manipulate opinions, create confusion, or influence decisions.
  • Example: Creating a fake news story to influence public opinion.

Malinformation

  • Genuine information shared to cause harm or damage.
  • It may involve private information or real content presented without appropriate context.
  • Example: Publishing someone's private messages to embarrass or harm them.

HOW DOES MISINFORMATION SPREAD?

  • Social media: Posts can reach thousands or millions of people quickly through sharing and recommendations.
  • Algorithms: Platforms recommend content based on interests and activity, potentially exposing people to similar claims repeatedly.
  • Emotional content: Shocking or frightening headlines can encourage people to share before checking accuracy.
  • Artificial intelligence: AI tools can create realistic fake images, videos, and audio recordings.
  • Private messaging: Rumours can spread through group chats and personal messages without being checked.

HOW TO SPOT MISINFORMATION

WHY IS IT A PROBLEM?

  • Public health: False medical claims can confuse people and affect health-related decisions.
  • Politics: Misleading claims can influence public understanding of elections and government decisions.
  • Trust: Repeated false information can weaken trust in journalism and reliable institutions.
  • Education: Students may use inaccurate information when researching school topics.
  • Personal relationships: Rumours and misleading posts can create arguments and damage reputations.

REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES

These examples demonstrate how misinformation and disinformation can affect real events and public understanding.

EXAMPLE 01 — PUBLIC HEALTH

The 5G and COVID-19 conspiracy theory

  • What happened: During the COVID-19 pandemic, false claims circulated online suggesting that 5G mobile networks caused or spread COVID-19.
  • What the evidence showed: Mobile networks cannot transmit viruses. COVID-19 also spread in places without 5G networks.
  • Why it matters: The false claims created confusion about the pandemic and distracted from reliable public-health information.
  • Source: European Commission — Fighting Disinformation.

EXAMPLE 02 — ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

AI-generated video impersonating Canada's Chief Public Health Officer

  • What happened: A manipulated video circulated online appearing to show Canada's Chief Public Health Officer, Dr. Theresa Tam, making false claims about COVID-19 vaccines.
  • What the evidence showed: AAP FactCheck identified the video as an AI-generated deepfake based on a genuine interview.
  • Why it matters: AI-generated media can imitate real people and make fabricated statements appear believable.
  • Source: AAP FactCheck.

EXAMPLE 03 — ELECTIONS

AI-generated Biden robocalls in New Hampshire

  • What happened: In January 2024, automated calls used an AI-generated imitation of President Joe Biden's voice to contact New Hampshire voters.
  • What the message said: The recording encouraged voters not to participate in the upcoming primary election.
  • What investigators found: The Federal Communications Commission issued a $6 million penalty against the person responsible.
  • Why it matters: Fake audio can impersonate public figures and potentially interfere with voters' understanding of elections.
  • Source: Federal Communications Commission — FCC 24-104.

EXAMPLE 04 — VACCINE MISINFORMATION

False claims about COVID-19 vaccines

  • What happened: False claims circulated online suggesting that COVID-19 vaccines could change a person's DNA or cause cancer.
  • What research found: Fact-checking investigations found that these claims were not supported by scientific evidence.
  • Why it matters: Repeated false health claims can create fear, confusion, and distrust in medical information.
  • Source: PolitiFact — COVID-19 Misinformation Persists.
